# 1.3 AI Agents and Their Environment
*AAI-501 · Week 1 · cross-checked against the transcript*

**One line:** An agent decides using **5 factors**, runs on
a **body + controller**, and you design it by choosing
across **10 dimensions.** Pick what fits the **task and
budget**, not the most complex.

**Running example:** the textbook's **lab delivery robot**
(delivers coffee, mail, lab equipment and supplies anywhere
in the lab building: outer rooms, central labs, main office).

## Review
- Agent = perceives via **sensors**, acts via
  **effectors/actuators.** Percepts come in, actions go out.
- Agents combine **perception, reasoning, action.**
- We focus on **rational** agents: choose the action that
  maximizes **expected outcome given information to date.**

## 5 factors in an agent's decision
(agent = "figurative and literal black box")

**Hook: A-G-P-S-P**

| Factor | Question | Generic example | Lab robot example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A**bilities | What am I capable of? Limits the decisions possible | Wants to swim underwater but has no scuba gear | Move forward/backward, pick up and set down objects, detect and avoid obstacles |
| **G**oals / preferences | What do I want? Differs per agent | Fast mail delivery vs accurate mail delivery -> different decisions | Deliver coffee to every scientist by 9 AM; avoid biohazard contamination; don't fall down the stairs |
| **P**rior knowledge | What do I know about the world? | Takes X time to open/close mailboxes; dirt roads have less traction -> plan extra time for rural routes | A **map of the lab** (where the stairs are); basic **physics** (gravity, momentum) behind the hardware |
| **S**timuli | What is happening **right now**? What sensors pick up | | **Slippery floor** (be careful); **scientists nearby** (avoid collision) |
| **P**ast experiences | What have I experienced? | | Real **battery range** (altitude, humidity cut the stated range); **carrying capacity** (how many coffee cups without spilling, weight and stability) |

**Prior knowledge vs past experience:** they overlap. Past
experience is the **richer picture** (what factors led to an
outcome). **Prior knowledge = the distillation of important
past experiences.**

## Inside the agent: body + controller
- **Body:** converts **stimuli -> percepts**, and
  **commands from the controller -> actions.**
  - Digital agents have a "body" too: e.g., an **API
    endpoint** that receives stimuli and sends API requests
    (web crawler).
- **Controller:** the **brain.** Takes percepts (all the
  factors above) and decides what to tell the body.
  **Machine learning algorithms live here.**
- **Belief state:** all information the agent **recalls from
  previous time**: specific facts, former positions/locations,
  **progress toward its goal.** Like memory.
- The controller is **responsible for maintaining the belief
  state AND deciding what to do.**

### Textbook Figure 2.4
- **Figure A (one step):** memory + percepts come into the
  controller. The **solid line = command function** (decide
  what to do from memory and percepts -> commands). The
  controller also **updates memory** from percepts, memory,
  and the decision.
- **Figure B (across time):** body + environment over **time
  steps T1, T2, T3, T4**. At each step the controller takes
  info from the past plus the body/environment, acts, and
  **passes info to the next step.** So the controller needs
  an effective understanding of **time** to keep a belief
  state and decide properly.

### Key point: ML is not an agent
Your ML algorithms **are not themselves AI agents.**
Example: a **linear regression** plotting blood pressure and
other health factors against risk scores helps doctors pick
a treatment, but **the people are the agent.** ML is used as
**part of the cognitive controller.** ML plays a functional
role in agents but is not an agent.

## Design space: 10 dimensions
- A design space = **10 dimensions of choices** that describe
  an agent's **complexity** and **how it views/understands the
  world.** Think **blueprint.**
- Choices dictate the **architecture and resource needs.**
- **Don't default to the most complex** design. Match **task
  and budget**; some tasks need more complexity, resources
  may be limited.

| # | Dimension | Question | Options |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Modularity** | Do we break thinking into smaller pieces? | flat, modular, hierarchical |
| 2 | **Planning horizon** | How far into the future? | non-planning, finite, indefinite, infinite |
| 3 | **Representation** | How do I describe my world? | states, features, relations |
| 4 | **Computational limits** | How much and for how long can I think? | perfect vs bounded rationality |
| 5 | **Learning** | Where does knowledge come from? | given vs learned |
| 6 | **Sensing uncertainty** | Do I have a complete view? | fully vs partially observable |
| 7 | **Effect uncertainty** | Do I know what my action causes? | deterministic vs stochastic |
| 8 | **Preference** | How do I define success? | goal vs complex preferences |
| 9 | **Number of agents** | Are there others in my world? | single, adversaries, multiple |
| 10 | **Interactivity** | When do I think? | online vs offline |

### 1. Modularity (like **microservices**)
Robot must plan its day: deliver coffee on time, but also
make time for longer trips, cleaning lab benches.
- **Flat:** no structure; all tasks considered together at
  **one level of abstraction.**
- **Modular:** break tasks into subtasks, e.g., *pick up
  coffee -> move from lab 261 to office 315 -> put down
  coffee.*
- **Hierarchical:** multiple modules, decomposed into simpler
  tasks at several levels. Deliver coffee -> *find location
  of target* + *use wheels to move there* -> even lower:
  *send an HTTP request to the wheels*, *motor control.*

### 2. Planning horizon
| Horizon | Meaning | Robot example |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-planning** | Doesn't consider the future | Reacting to **bug alerts** from internal systems: just surface and process alerts |
| **Finite** | Plan a **set number** of steps/time steps | **Battery life:** only 3 hours of work before recharging |
| **Indefinite** | Look ahead a **finite but unknown** number of steps | Deliver coffee to **scientist Dave** (out until noon; robot searches room to room). Will happen eventually, unknown when |
| **Infinite** | Plan **perpetually** | **Staying upright** at all times (stability mechanism) |

### 3. Representation (how do I describe my world?)
- **States:** snapshots; **every possible configuration** of
  the world (e.g., lab map + location of every scientist).
  Problem: **state space explodes** quickly.
- **Features:** condense the space. E.g., instead of "which
  room is each scientist in" as states, use **Boolean
  features** ("is Dave in room 101?", "is Susie in room 101?").
  **30 features can describe over 1 billion states.**
- **Relations:** relationships/network, e.g., "has coffee been
  delivered to Dave?", "is my direct path clear?". Good for
  **social media / LinkedIn connections.**
- **Only represent the relevant part of the world.** A robot
  that stays inside needn't model outside weather or the
  color of Dave's t-shirt.

### 4. Computational limits
- **Perfect rationality:** think until you reach the
  **perfect solution.**
- **Bounded rationality:** decisions made under constraints:
  - **Resource constraints** (limited processing power)
  - **Situational constraints** (e.g., avoid colliding with
    Dave)
- Example: a perfectly optimal path around Dave is useless if
  it takes so long to compute that the **collision already
  happened.** Collision avoidance needs bounded rationality.

### 5. Learning
- **Given:** knowledge programmed in (**rules-based systems**,
  like the expert systems from 1.2).
- **Learned:** an internal **ML model** learns. E.g., **slippery
  floors are a function of shininess** (shinier = riskier), or
  **where each person spends time** and **when they ask for
  coffee.**

### 6. Sensing uncertainty
- **Fully observable:** sees everything at all times
  ("omniscient"). E.g., robot in a **security control booth**
  watching video feeds of the whole lab.
- **Partially observable:** can't see/know some things. E.g.,
  robot and scientist walking toward the **same corridor
  corner**: neither can see through walls.

### 7. Effect uncertainty
- **Deterministic:** you can exactly foresee the outcome.
  E.g., **knocking over dominoes** (gravity). Even if a
  scientist might interfere, for practical purposes
  deterministic.
- **Stochastic:** outcome not certain. E.g., **stock trading**
  (go up or down, no guarantees).

### 8. Preference (how is success defined?)
- **Goal:** a simple outcome to make true: in a **final state**
  (deliver coffee to Dave) **or across time** (stay upright
  at all times).
- **Complex preferences:** **trade-offs between outcomes.**
  E.g., coffee for Dave and a letter for Susie in different
  rooms: which first?

### 9. Number of agents
- **Single agent:** the only entity to consider.
- **Adversaries:** another agent **competing** with you for
  the task. **Zero-sum:** you or them.
- **Multiple agents:** others you must **plan around but not
  compete with.**
- **"Is it an agent?" test:** do I need to **take its
  reasoning into account?**
  - Bird outside (robot never goes out): **no**
  - Another delivery robot: **yes** (divide and conquer
    deliveries; avoid hallway collisions)
  - Scientists: **yes** (their reasoning, even their grouchiness
    before coffee)
- Adversary vs multiple agents have **vastly different
  algorithmic and design implications.**

### 10. Interactivity
- **Online:** compute, update understanding, and plan **while
  engaging with the world** (real time).
- **Offline:** compute/updates happen **outside** engagement,
  e.g., only processes while **docked for the night.**
- Online is very powerful but puts **big demands on the agent,
  compute, and infrastructure.**

## Practical note
You don't redefine the design space from scratch. Choose a
**template** for the problem (web crawler, AI chatbot,
self-driving car). Use domain knowledge to simplify.
